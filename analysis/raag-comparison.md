## Why is Raag Bhairav most similar to Makams

https://raag-hindustani.com/Scales5.html

The core reason Raag Bhairav feels tonally proximate to the Turkish makam tradition is that it shares the same fundamental interval instinct: the lowered second degree as an expressive, gravitational force

This ancient and very important raga is difficult for many reasons, but the reason I want to focus on here is its use of microtones which directly maps to makams. The note re (♭2) in this raga is extra flat, which makes the interval between the notes Sa (1) and re (♭2) extremely small and takes skill to execute. (Note the difference between the re in this raga and the same note in Raag Todi above.)

In Bhairav, the komal re (flat 2nd) and komal dha (flat 6th) are not just scale tones, they're the defining emotional centers of the raag. The movement from komal re down to Sa, or the lingering on komal dha, creates a kind of tender, aching pull toward the tonic. 

This is precisely the interval logic that makam thinking is built around. Turkish modal music is obsessed with how a pitch leans toward another, how a half-step approach generates tension and resolution, and especially how a minor second below a resting tone creates a sense of yearning. Bhairav's entire melodic personality lives in exactly that half step tension.

![bhairav](https://github.com/Sangarshanan/makam-and-ragas/blob/main/analysis/media/bhairav1.png)

A second structural convergence is the presence of what you might call asymmetric tetrachords. Bhairav divides the octave into two unequal halves: the lower tetrachord (S r G m) contains a minor second followed by a major third, while the upper (P d N S) mirrors that with a similar shape. Turkish makam theory is fundamentally tetrachordal — makams are constructed by stacking and combining tetrachords (called cins), and many of the most characteristic makams use exactly this kind of asymmetric, half-step-loaded lower tetrachord to generate their distinctive color. Bhairav's tetrachordal structure would feel familiar to that grammar even without sharing exact pitches.

## Why Raag Todi is furthest from makams

Todi's scale is: S r g m↑ P d N S — flat 2nd, flat 3rd, sharp 4th, flat 6th. That's four altered degrees, making it one of the most chromatically dense raags in the entire Hindustani system.

![tetrachords](https://github.com/Sangarshanan/makam-and-ragas/blob/main/analysis/media/todi1.png)

The flat 2nd, flat 3rd, sharp 4th, flat 6th simultaneously produces an interval structure that doesn't map cleanly onto any single makam tetrachordal grammar. Makam theory builds scales by stacking recognized tetrachord types. Todi's lower tetrachord (Sa → ♭2 → ♭3 → ♯4) spanning a tritone is not a standard cins. It's a highly compressed, idiosyncratic unit that Hindustani theory treats as a distinctive whole rather than a stackable building block

There's also something important about Todi's melodic personality that pushes it further from the makam world than Bhairav. In Bhairav, the komal re and komal dha are the expressive centers but the melodic movement is relatively fluid. In Todi, the melody tends to dwell intensely in the lower register hovering between Sa, komal re, and komal ga in a way that feels almost obsessive. That extreme registral compression and the aching quality of the flat 2nd and flat 3rd rubbing against each other is something quite specific to the Hindustani aesthetic. It's recognizably tense to a makam listener, but the kind of tension is different: less like yearning (which makam does well) and more like a kind of grief-saturated stillness.
